Expansion Is Already on the Way, So Why Won't Memory Get Cheaper Anytime Soon

Aug 12, 2026

The pressure from consumer electronics is showing up in end-device pricing and shipment expectations. Both IDC and Gartner’s models suggest that rising memory costs will push up the average selling price of PCs and smartphones while reducing shipment volumes. The specific figures and models differ between the two, so they cannot be combined into a single unified conclusion. This naturally raises a question: aren’t memory manufacturers all expanding capacity? When will memory prices come down?

As of August 12, 2026, the answer based on publicly available information is not straightforward: The second half of 2026 looks more like a slowdown in price increases rather than a broad-based price decline; the second half of 2027 is one of the earliest windows in which NAND Flash supply and demand may gradually ease, but it is neither a DRAM price-cut schedule nor a guarantee for retail prices of PCs, smartphones, and SSDs.

The scope of discussion here is limited to DRAM, NAND Flash, and the PCs, phones, and storage products whose costs are pulled by them. It should not be extrapolated into “when will all semiconductors drop in price”: mature process nodes, logic foundry, MCUs, analog chips, and advanced packaging each have their own supply and demand cycles.

Hong Kong brokerages will not be revalued together, but will be re-stratified.

Aug 12, 2026

Looking at the three recent capital moves over the past few months together, the changes in Hong Kong brokerages become much clearer.

In March, an Ant-affiliated buyer completed the acquisition of approximately 50.55% of the shares in Yaocai Securities. On August 6, CITIC Securities completed a targeted H-share issuance, with all proceeds retained overseas for developing its international business. One day later, the offeror under Guotai Haitong submitted a proposal to privatize Guotai Junan International at HK$3 per share.

The latest transaction, as of August 12, 2026, remains a proposal with attached preconditions and not a completed privatization. These three deals are also not the same type of transaction: one purchases an operating foothold in a local brokerage, one supplements the balance sheet for cross-border business, and one attempts to regain full control of a listed subsidiary. They do not prove that industry concentration has risen, but they provide three observation windows into what capabilities large capital is willing to pay for.

2ms WAN Connection: Are Exchanges "Slowing Down the Accelerator for Quant Trading"?

Jul 31, 2026

The “Technical Requirements for Wide Area Network Trading and Market Data Lines” recently discussed in the market, the most striking sentence is: the wide area network lines used for trading and market data services must have a bidirectional latency of no less than 2ms. According to reports, the original local area network trading and market data lines within the data center will also be phased out, and access will be uniformly transitioned to wide area network lines.

This sounds like slapping a speed limit on the fastest car. But it would be a stretch to conclude that “quant trading is banned” or that “retail traders now move at the same speed as institutions.” It’s more of a reordering at the access layer: replacing what were originally physical shortcuts potentially created by colocation with a manageable, dedicated wide-area channel that all participants must use. It will change a small class of strategies that are extremely sensitive to microseconds and milliseconds, but it won’t rewrite matching priority, nor will it eliminate the differences along the entire trading chain.

C++ Singleton: Initialization Guarantees Behind a Single `static` Line

Jul 24, 2026

Many C++ singleton implementations eventually shrink to this:

static T& Instance() {
    // C++11 guarantees thread-safe initialization of function-local static objects.
    static T instance;
    return instance;
}

It looks like hiding an object inside a function, and using a reference to retrieve it. What is truly worth discussing is not the singleton pattern itself, but the fact that this code simultaneously touches upon C++ storage duration, scope, references, deferred initialization, and the concurrency semantics that were only completed in C++11.

First, a correction on the terminology: the “C98” mentioned in the context should be “C++98”. Strictly speaking, C has no version called C98; more importantly, C does not have C++’s classes, references, or member functions, so the T& syntax is not C code at all.
