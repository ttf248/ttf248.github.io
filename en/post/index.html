Family Planning Thinned Kinship Networks, but Did Not Decide Who Built a Dynasty

Sep 09, 2026

Fewer children mean a thinner kinship network, but not necessarily more dispersed wealth. A couple’s property and savings may end up concentrated in the hands of their only child. That counterintuitive possibility is the first obstacle to clear when discussing family planning and the power of families.

Family planning can change how many branches a family has. It cannot, by itself, explain who can pass down assets, advantages associated with official positions, and social connections. Drawing a direct line between China’s population policy and America’s wealthy dynasties conflates three questions: population size, property institutions and political organization.

Tencent’s reports tell a less orderly story. Older products keep earning, new ones seek growth, and mobile, PC, domestic and international businesses move at different speeds.

The more useful question is why a player chooses to pay again. Items, cosmetics, seasonal rewards and in-app purchases in mini-programs represent different relationships with a product. Financial reports show how the business grows, but do not necessarily trace the same person moving between games. Apple rankings offer a platform snapshot; they cannot supply genre revenue that the company has not disclosed.

The July 2026 announcement on offshore-trust taxation brought a wealth arrangement that can seem remote from everyday life down to specific questions: who contributed the assets, who earned the income, which year requires a filing, and what records establish the facts?

It does not mean China’s tax authorities are taking over overseas trustees, or that every overseas financial product suddenly faces a new tax. It extends a direction visible in two decades of administrative reform: where assets are held is no longer sufficient on its own to explain the tax relationship. Tax residence, actual control and the attribution of income also matter.

The Old Economics of the Memory Industry

Aug 14, 2026

Rising memory and SSD prices make the profitable side of the memory business easy to see. These products are almost unavoidable in modern computing, the technical barriers are high, and new factories are expensive. Manufacturers might therefore seem entitled to a good business for years to come.

Yet bankruptcies, sales and restructurings are just as prominent in its history. Elpida and Qimonda both had technology, factories and patents; neither survived as the same independent competitor. The question is not whether memory can make money. It is whether profits in a good market can pay for the next technology generation, capacity expansion and the next downturn.

Capacity Expansion Is Underway. Why Won't Memory Get Cheaper Soon?

Aug 12, 2026

Consumer electronics are already feeling the cost of more expensive memory, even as announcements of new capacity keep arriving. Put the two together, and it is tempting to think that phones, PCs, and SSDs should become cheaper once a few more factories open.

The difficulty lies in what happens after they open. New manufacturing capacity must turn into qualified products, those products must reach the consumer market, and only then might prices on store shelves change. Expansion adds manufacturing capability; consumers are waiting for usable supply of the products they need. The distance between the two deserves more attention than the size of the investment.

As of August 12, 2026, the available material supports a limited conclusion: the second half of 2026 looks more like a period of slower price increases; the second half of 2027 is one possible window for NAND supply and demand to ease gradually. There is no equally clear window for DRAM, and no promise of cheaper PCs, phones, or retail SSDs. This discussion concerns memory and storage chips, not all semiconductors.
