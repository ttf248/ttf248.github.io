Why is Hexakill ARAM so popular, and how many players does regular ARAM still have?

抖音直播只是放大器，真正让它活下来的，是每一局都能重新发明玩法

The problem with regular ARAM isn’t that it’s not fun—it’s that it’s too easy to understand. When a streamer picks a champion, viewers can quickly predict how the game will play out: what items to build, which skills to rely on, and when the power spikes happen. After watching several rounds, the variation mainly comes from the heroes and teammates, while the gameplay itself rarely offers any surprises.

On February 6, 2024, League of Legends livestreaming was fully opened on Douyin. The licensing opened the doorway, but it did not automatically produce appealing livestream content. What Hex Faction (ARAM) later added was precisely what livestream channels needed most: in every match, there exists a question of “what will happen next.”

Normal Arena only randomizes the champions, while Hextech randomizes the entire script

The randomness of a normal ARAM mainly occurs during the champion selection phase. Once the champion is determined, players still generally follow the familiar abilities, builds, and roles. After playing a champion a few times, the audience can recognize its fixed rhythm.

ARAM: Mayhem extends the random concept further. It keeps ARAM’s single-lane 5v5 structure while adding augment selections. At launch, the rules were that you choose one augment from three at the start, then continue choosing at levels 7, 11, and 15; like items, you can still return to the shop after dying to collect your augments. Riot’s description of the mode in the 25.21 patch notes states it plainly: this is Arena’s augments stuffed into the familiar ARAM.

This brings about two different types of content changes:

Classic ARAM Hextech ARAM
Main Per-Game Variation Champions, teammates, and team composition Champions, augments, items, and ability paths
What Viewers Care About Most How strong is this champion What does this augment transform the champion into
Memorable Moments of a Game Plays, composition, and team fights Combos, power spikes, and absurd comebacks
Why You Want to Watch the Next Game Try a different champion Gamble on a new combo again

So the core of Hex isn’t simply “more randomness,” but the stacking of hero randomness, augment randomness, and on-the-fly decision-making. The same hero might be a familiar ranged poke champion today, but in the next game, because of an augment, could become a melee frontline, a sustained damage machine, or a champion that repeatedly triggers effects through a certain ability. Players aren’t just using a hero — they’re temporarily designing that hero.

The key is that it doesn’t hand all the decision-making power to the dice. Typically, multiple options are presented each time, and players must choose between “immediate strength,” “future build potential,” and “whether it synergizes with teammates.” Randomness creates problems, choices provide a sense of participation, and combined results deliver feedback. This structure makes people more willing to queue up repeatedly than simply receiving a random buff.

What live streaming really needs is what happens in the next minute

The demands of the livestream audience and the players are not entirely the same. Players can afford to let a match unfold gradually, but viewers often drop in mid-game, giving the streamer only a few dozen seconds to prove that “there’s something worth watching here.”

Teamfight Tactics conveniently divides a match into several easy-to-understand phases: which champion you get, which first augmentation you pick, whether your mid-game composition comes together, and finally whether you can execute that playstyle. The augmentation choice itself is a small cliffhanger, while combat is responsible for delivering on that suspense.

Its live streaming value roughly comes from four moments:

  • The Moment of Choice: The streamer explains which of the three augmentations better fits the current hero, and viewers can participate in making the judgment via bullet comments.
  • The Moment of Formation: After the second and third augmentations appear, the previously vague idea begins to crystallize into a playstyle that can be articulated.
  • The Moment of Payoff: Skill range, attack patterns, movement speed, or survivability suddenly change, and viewers can see the difference even without understanding all the numbers.
  • The Moment of Failure: The combination fails to come together, or an augment that looks strong doesn’t synergize with the hero. The streamer needs to explain on the spot why this gamble didn’t pay off.

This is also why it is more suitable for short video clips than regular ARAM. The highlights of regular ARAM are often concentrated on a single play, while Hex ARAM can be edited as a complete mini-story with the full “choose—wait—cash in” flow. After watching one combo, viewers will naturally have another question: if I switch to a different champion, would this augmentation still work?

The opening of copyright on Douyin Live addresses whether content can enter the platform; HeXi addresses whether, after content enters the platform, it can continuously generate new reasons to watch. The former is a distribution entry point, the latter is a content engine. Conflating the two will lead to an overestimation of the role of copyright; separating them reveals why the same kind of live-streamed brawls can produce entirely different results.

It Transforms Imbalance into a Satisfying Experience

Traditional competitive modes generally aim to reduce extremes: heroes are kept close in strength, item builds are stable, and wins and losses are determined more by mechanics and decision-making. ARAM / Arena flips that on its head by embracing one thing: some games are supposed to feature wildly overpowered combinations.

This does not mean the development team is giving up on balance. Riot explicitly stated in a follow-up dev blog that balancing Hextech augments is first and foremost about fun and freshness, while still holding firm on a few bottom lines: every champion should have worthwhile and powerful builds to pursue, the strongest build shouldn’t be reliably obtainable every game, and a powerful build alone shouldn’t be able to single-handedly determine the outcome. The ARAM Mayhem Dev Blog provides a complete explanation of these trade-offs.

This kind of trade-offs works well for live streaming. Strict balance would reduce the “outrageous moments,” which are precisely what live streams most easily turn into shared memories. A skill suddenly gaining an extra projectile, or a previously fragile hero becoming a monster that can hold the frontline, are all things that streamers can explain clearly, viewers can wait for, and editors can clip and share.

Of course, imbalance also feeds back into the gameplay itself. The side that high-rolls may enter “showtime” early, while the side that low-rolls can only wait for the next round; when everyone discovers that certain combos are the easiest path to victory, the original open exploration turns into yet another fixed answer. Riot later removed the Augment system, and one of the stated reasons was that the different Augments were increasingly converging on similar experiences, with Augment effects even overshadowing the champions themselves. The Patch 26.12 notes then added Ability Augments and Quest Augments, putting the variability back onto specific champions and specific abilities.

This perfectly illustrates the design challenge for Hex: it cannot just increase the number of augments, but must continuously protect the feeling that “this game is different from the last one.” Otherwise, the more augments are added, the faster players will find the optimal solution, and the livestream will revert to repetitive scripts.

From activity mode to residency, it depends on a content update chain

Hexes were indeed initially treated as a limited-time mode. When 25.21 went live, the official notes stated it would only be available through 25.24; but by 2026, the product direction has shifted: it has begun to have its own progression, enhancement categories, and an ongoing update schedule.

Time What Happened Product Signal Revealed
October 2025 25.21 launched, officially positioned as a limited-time mode First validate demand through an event
January 2026 Delayed removal due to player feedback The operation cycle no longer ended as originally planned
February 2026 26.3 added 45 augments, augment sets, and mode progression Beginning to build long-term content and progression lines
March 2026 Esports media reported Riot confirmed long-term retention The “limited-time” label was removed
After May 2026 Official announcement of 26.12 ability/mission augments, with content planned for 2026 and 2027 The mode has an independent update roadmap
September 2026 26.18 still adjusting augment pool and champion compatibility Already being maintained as a long-term mode

The patch 26.3 announcement called this update a major Hextech update, explicitly mentioning the addition of 45 new augments, augment sets, and a progression system. The May dev log further framed the goal as “rotation and evolution,” ensuring that each major update brings different systems. Read together, the 26.3 patch notes, 26.12 patch notes, and the current 26.18 patch notes show that Hextech is no longer a one-time event, but rather a product line that can be continuously expanded with new content.

The challenge of an activity pattern is that the first week relies on novelty, the second week relies on friends, and from the third week onward it depends on new content-driven reasons. Hextech has turned this reason into a system: the enhancement pool can be swapped, champion synergies can be adjusted, combo strategies can be updated, and every time a streamer returns, there’s a new “meta answer” to test. It doesn’t sell the same set of gameplay repeatedly for a long time—instead, it equips the gameplay itself with a seasonal refresh mechanism.

1. Content Conjecture

Based on the title, this article likely discusses a strategy of “分流” (splitting/diversion) in team fights or battles. The core conjecture is that by splitting enemy attention or forces, even though standard chaotic team fights still exist, players effectively create more controlled engagement scenarios. The word “大型” here may refer to “大型团战” (large-scale team fights) or could be a stylistic emphasis.

Let me make this clear upfront: Normal ARAM has not disappeared, and Hextech ARAM does indeed have many people playing it. As for whether Hextech is currently more popular than Normal ARAM, public sources can support that it was “likely noticeably more popular for a period of time,” but cannot support an exact current ratio.

Howling Abyss (Classic ARAM) has been a separate queue from the beginning. When Riot introduced Mayhem, they specifically stated that Mayhem would queue separately, and players who don’t like augments can still play the original ARAM. The official dev blog also treats the two experiences as parallel offerings. As a result, classic ARAM continues to serve a clear group of players: those who prefer the way champions are designed to play, who want more readable team fights, or who simply don’t want a match’s outcome to be disproportionately decided by augment combinations.

Regarding the number of people, public evidence can be divided into three layers:

Question What can currently be confirmed What cannot be directly inferred
Is normal ARAM still being played It remains a separate queue, and Riot continues to maintain this experience “Being able to find matches” does not indicate the specific number of players
Are a lot of people playing Hextech ARAMGG collected 1,879,022 complete matches for patch 26.16, of which approximately 96.7% came from public samples in mainland China This is a third-party sample, not Riot’s total server-wide data
Does Hextech have more players than normal ARAM A community estimate suggests that patch 25.23 had approximately 78.25 million Hextech matches and approximately 30.63 million normal ARAM matches, with an estimated range of roughly 58 million to 86 million matches This is not an official statistic, and it only corresponds to a historical patch, so it cannot be treated as an accurate share for today
How are these two game numbers calculated

This estimate is not read directly from Riot’s full API. Instead, it derives the total ARAM matches from the historical relationship between Summoner’s Rift and regular ARAM, then subtracts regular ARAM. Using its median estimate, 78249630 ÷ 30630070 ≈ 2.56; the original author also provides a range of approximately 58 million to 86 million matches. This figure is suitable for illustrating the scale of Heimerdinger’s (Hextech) launch period, and should not be used as a real-time proportion for September 2026.

The conclusions from the table above are sufficient to answer the practical question: Hex isn’t only played by streamers—it has a real volume of matches large enough to support an independent stats site and a version ecosystem; normal Arena isn’t dead either, it’s just that some players seeking novelty and high variance have been drawn away to Hex.

A boundary should also be drawn here. Riot does not publicly disclose real-time player counts broken down by region and queue, and third-party websites typically only cover the matches they are able to collect. Therefore, when you see claims online about “what percentage of all ARAM games Hextech makes up,” you must first check whether it is full data, sampled data, or an estimate inferred from another mode. What can be confirmed is the direction; it would be dishonest to pretend that the exact share can be confirmed.

Two Types of Brawls, Two Kinds of Fun

Normal ARAM sells a stable sense of companionship. The champions are random, but the rules are familiar; the wins and losses may not be fair, yet they remain easy to understand. It suits players who play on their own, as well as those who enjoy studying champions, practicing team fights, and reducing system noise.

What Hex Arena Arena sells is the feeling of discovery. It lets players temporarily invent a hero, lets streamers temporarily invent a piece of content, and lets viewers temporarily take part in a gamble. The repetition of normal Arena comes from the hero scripts; the repetition of Hex, however, is pushed back until after “I haven’t seen the next combination yet.”

So I’d rather think of Hex as a content machine than as a simple upgrade to a regular brawl. What makes it most successful is not just that it makes a single game stronger and more chaotic, but that it connects choices, combinations, payoff, and spread into a loop. Douyin livestreams amplify this loop, and player feedback in turn drives the augments pool to keep evolving.

Regular brawls will of course still exist. But from the livestream perspective, Hex augments are easier to spot; from the game product perspective, Hex augments are also more worth continuously updating. It answers not “how many more times can a single champion be played,” but rather “how many more times can the same champion be reinvented.”
